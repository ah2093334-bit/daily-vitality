Vitamin B12: Food Sources, Deficiency Basics, and When Testing May Help

Educational wellness reading•Practical tools linked below

Quick overview

Vitamin B12 supports red blood cell formation and normal nervous-system function. Low levels can develop slowly, especially when intake or absorption is reduced, so persistent symptoms deserve context rather than guesswork.

What this article covers

  • What vitamin B12 does
  • Foods that contain B12
  • Who may be at higher risk of low levels
  • Why symptoms alone cannot confirm deficiency

What B12 does in the body

Vitamin B12 is involved in red blood cell production, DNA processes and nerve function. Because those jobs affect many systems, low levels can show up in ways that feel nonspecific.

Fatigue, weakness, numbness or tingling, and certain blood changes can have many causes, so symptoms should not be used as a self-diagnosis.

Food sources and absorption

Animal foods such as meat, fish, eggs and dairy naturally contain B12. Some plant-based foods are fortified. People eating a strict vegan diet often need a reliable fortified source or supplement plan.

Absorption can also be affected by age, stomach or intestinal conditions, and some medicines, which is why intake alone does not tell the whole story.

When testing may help

Testing is most useful when symptoms, diet pattern, medicines or medical history create a real reason to check. A clinician can interpret the result together with blood counts and other markers when needed.

If neurological symptoms are new or worsening, do not rely on an online checklist alone.
