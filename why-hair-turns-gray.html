Why Hair Turns Gray: The Science of Melanin and Aging

Daily Vitality · 5 min read

Gray hair isn't hair losing color — it's hair growing in without any color to begin with. Here's why that starts happening.

← scroll for more →

Where Hair Color Actually Comes From

Each hair follicle contains pigment-producing cells called melanocytes, clustered at the base of the follicle in a structure called the hair bulb. These cells inject pigment (melanin) into each strand of hair as it grows — more melanin makes darker hair, less makes it lighter.

Hair FollicleThe tube-shaped structure in the skin from which each hair strand grows.

Worth knowing: By around age 50, most people have lost roughly half of the melanocyte activity in their hair follicles — which is why gray hair tends to accelerate noticeably in that decade for many people.

Why Melanocytes Slow Down

As you age, melanocyte stem cells gradually deplete and become less active, producing less pigment or stopping altogether — new hair grows in without color, appearing gray or white. Genetics largely determines when this starts, which is why greying age runs strongly in families. Chronic stress, smoking, and certain nutrient deficiencies (like vitamin B12) have also been linked to earlier greying, though genetics remains the dominant factor.

Can Anything Slow It Down?

There's no proven way to reverse greying once melanocyte activity has stopped in a follicle. However, addressing deficiencies (particularly B12, iron, and copper) if present, managing chronic stress, and avoiding smoking may help delay premature greying in some cases. Beyond that, greying at the expected age for your family is simply a normal part of aging, not a sign of a health problem.
