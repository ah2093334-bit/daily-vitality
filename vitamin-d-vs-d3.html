Vitamin D vs. Vitamin D3: What’s the Difference?

Vitamin D2 and D3 are two forms of the same essential nutrient. Both can raise vitamin D levels, but they are not completely identical.

Daily Vitality Editorial Team•Updated September 22, 2026•Educational, non-diagnostic
About this article: This page is independently written for Daily Vitality and uses the public health sources listed below. It is not presented as a substitute for diagnosis or personalized medical care.

What vitamin D actually does

Vitamin D helps the body absorb calcium and supports normal bone, muscle, nerve, and immune function. Most people get vitamin D through a mix of food, fortified foods, sunlight, and sometimes supplements.

D2 vs. D3 in plain English

Vitamin D2 is called ergocalciferol and vitamin D3 is called cholecalciferol. Both forms raise vitamin D in the blood. NIH guidance notes that D3 may raise blood levels somewhat higher and keep them elevated for longer than D2. That does not mean everyone needs D3 supplements; the right choice depends on diet, health history, test results, and professional advice.

Where vitamin D comes from

Fatty fish such as salmon and trout are among the best natural food sources. Fortified milk and many plant-based alternatives can also contribute. Egg yolks, cheese, liver, and some UV-exposed mushrooms contain smaller amounts.

Should you rely on sunlight?

Your skin can make vitamin D from sunlight, but cloud cover, skin pigmentation, age, season, clothing, and location all affect production. Sun exposure also carries skin-cancer risk, so vitamin D is not a reason to skip normal sun-protection advice.

When a blood test matters

A 25-hydroxyvitamin D blood test is the standard way clinicians assess vitamin D status. Testing can be useful when deficiency is suspected or when a clinician is monitoring treatment. Symptoms alone cannot reliably tell you your vitamin D level.

A practical supplement rule

Avoid treating “more” as automatically better. Vitamin D is fat-soluble, and excessive intake can be harmful. If you take a supplement, consider your total intake from food and other products and discuss higher-dose use with a clinician or pharmacist.
