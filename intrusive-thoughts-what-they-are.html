Intrusive Thoughts: What They Are and When to Get Help

Intrusive thoughts are unwanted thoughts, images, or urges that can feel upsetting precisely because they do not match what a person wants or values.

Daily Vitality Editorial Team•Updated September 22, 2026•Educational, non-diagnostic
About this article: This page is independently written for Daily Vitality and uses the public health sources listed below. It is not presented as a substitute for diagnosis or personalized medical care.

An intrusive thought is not an intention

People can have strange, unwanted, or upsetting thoughts without wanting to act on them. The presence of a thought by itself does not define your character or predict what you will do.

Why trying to “force it away” can backfire

Constantly checking whether a thought is gone, arguing with it, or seeking repeated reassurance can sometimes keep attention locked onto it. A gentler response is to notice the thought, avoid treating it as a command, and return attention to the task in front of you.

When intrusive thoughts are part of OCD

NIMH describes obsessions in OCD as recurring, unwanted thoughts, urges, or mental images that can cause significant anxiety. OCD also involves compulsions for many people — repetitive behaviors or mental acts used to reduce distress.

When to talk with a professional

Consider professional help if intrusive thoughts are consuming large amounts of time, causing major distress, interfering with school, work, sleep, relationships, or daily activities, or leading to repetitive checking or rituals.

What support can look like

Evidence-based mental-health treatment can help people manage OCD and related anxiety. A licensed mental-health professional can assess the full picture and discuss appropriate options.

If there is immediate danger

If you feel at risk of acting on thoughts of harming yourself or someone else, seek urgent local emergency help or crisis support rather than relying on an online article or self-test.
