Creatine After 40: What the New Evidence Actually Says

What current evidence says about creatine, resistance training, strength and healthy aging—without turning a supplement into a promise.

Why creatine is back in the conversation

Creatine is no longer discussed only in bodybuilding circles. Research published in 2026 continues to examine whether creatine adds useful benefits when older adults perform resistance training. A recent systematic review found a modest improvement in muscle strength when creatine was combined with resistance training, while evidence for extra gains in muscle mass and physical function remained uncertain.

Training still does the heavy lifting

A supplement cannot replace progressive resistance training, adequate food or recovery. A sensible plan can begin with two or three weekly sessions covering major movement patterns—such as a squat or sit-to-stand, a push, a pull, a hinge and a carry—adapted to ability. Progress gradually rather than testing limits every session.

What the 2026 evidence suggests

The newest systematic review of adults 60 and older included 11 randomized trials. The added effect on strength was small but statistically significant; the authors did not find clear additional benefits for muscle mass or physical function, and the certainty for those outcomes was low or very low. That distinction matters: “may help strength” is very different from “guarantees more muscle.”

Safety and individual context

Creatine is a supplement, not a treatment for a medical condition. Anyone with kidney disease, pregnancy, a complex medication list or another medical concern should discuss supplements with a qualified clinician before starting. Choose products with transparent labeling rather than proprietary blends.

A better takeaway

If your goal is healthy aging, put the order of operations first: consistent resistance training, enough food and protein for your situation, sleep, and regular movement. Then consider whether a supplement has a reasonable evidence-to-risk ratio for you. Creatine can be part of that conversation, but it should not become the entire plan.

Educational note: This article is general wellness information, not a diagnosis or personal medical advice. If symptoms are severe, persistent or concerning, speak with a qualified healthcare professional.
