Creatine: Not Just for Athletes Anymore

By Wafadar • Updated September 2, 2026 • 7 min read
💡 Quick Summary
  • Creatine is one of the most researched supplements available, historically used mainly by athletes
  • Newer research interest includes its role in muscle maintenance as we age and general energy metabolism
  • It's generally well-tolerated, but isn't automatically right for everyone

For years, creatine had a narrow reputation as a bodybuilder's supplement. That's changed — it's now one of the most talked-about additions to everyday wellness routines, for reasons that go beyond the gym.

What Creatine Actually Is

Creatine is a compound the body naturally produces and also gets from foods like red meat and fish. It's stored primarily in muscle tissue, where it helps regenerate a molecule called ATP — the body's primary energy currency for short bursts of effort.

How It Works in the Body

By supporting quicker ATP regeneration, creatine can help with short, high-intensity efforts — think sprinting or lifting — more than with long, steady endurance activity. It draws water into muscle cells as part of this process, which is part of why some people notice a small increase in body weight when starting it.

📌
Important NoteCreatine is one of the most extensively studied supplements available, but "well-studied" doesn't automatically mean "right for everyone" — individual health factors still matter.

Why It's Trending Beyond the Gym

Growing interest in healthy aging has brought attention to creatine's potential role in maintaining muscle mass and strength as people get older — a factor connected to long-term mobility and independence. Some emerging research interest also looks at brain energy metabolism, though this area is less established than its role in muscle.

Who's Commonly Using It Now

Beyond competitive athletes, it's increasingly discussed among older adults focused on maintaining strength, and among general fitness enthusiasts as part of a broader routine — though individual goals and appropriate use vary widely.

Things Worth Knowing Before Starting

Adequate water intake is commonly recommended alongside creatine use, given how it draws water into muscle cells. Reported side effects are generally mild for most healthy adults, most commonly digestive discomfort when taking larger amounts at once.

Who Should Check With a Doctor First

People with existing kidney conditions, anyone on medications that affect kidney function, and anyone pregnant or breastfeeding should talk to a doctor before starting — the kidneys are involved in processing creatine byproducts, which is why this conversation matters most for people with existing kidney concerns.

This article is for general educational purposes and is not a substitute for professional medical advice.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does creatine hurt your kidneys?

In healthy individuals with normal kidney function, current research generally hasn't shown kidney damage from typical use — but anyone with existing kidney issues should check with a doctor first.

Is creatine only for building muscle?

Building strength is its most established use, though interest in other potential benefits, like supporting muscle maintenance with age, is a growing area of research.

Do women need it differently than men?

Interest in creatine among women has grown, and general research doesn't show a fundamentally different response — but individual needs and appropriate use should still be discussed with a doctor or dietitian.
