Vitamins and Minerals: What Your Body Needs

By Wafadar • Updated August 2026 • 6 min read
💡 Quick Summary
  • Vitamin D, B12, iron, calcium, and magnesium are among the most commonly low nutrients
  • Mild deficiencies often cause vague symptoms like fatigue that get blamed on stress
  • A varied diet covers most needs — supplements are best guided by an actual blood test

Vitamins and minerals do not provide energy directly, but they are essential for nearly every process in your body, from healing wounds to keeping your heart beating steadily.

Key Vitamins Worth Knowing

Vitamin D supports bone health and immune function, but many people are low on it since it mainly comes from sunlight, not food. Vitamin B12 is essential for nerve function and red blood cells, and is only found naturally in animal products, making it a common gap for plant-based eaters.

Key Minerals Worth Knowing

Iron carries oxygen in your blood; low levels cause fatigue and are common, especially in women. Calcium supports bone strength throughout life, not just in childhood. Magnesium is involved in hundreds of bodily processes, from muscle function to sleep quality.

Why Deficiencies Often Go Unnoticed

Mild deficiencies frequently cause vague symptoms like fatigue or low mood, which are easy to attribute to stress instead. This is why unexplained, persistent fatigue is worth checking with a simple blood test.

⚠️
Common MistakeAttributing ongoing fatigue purely to "being busy" or stress without ever checking basic nutrient levels.

Common Food Sources

✅ Helpful
  • Leafy greens (iron, calcium)
  • Fatty fish (vitamin D, B12)
  • Citrus fruits (vitamin C)
  • Nuts and seeds (magnesium)
  • Eggs and dairy (B12, calcium)
❌ Best Avoided
  • Vitamin D (limited sun exposure)
  • B12 (plant-based diets without supplementation)
  • Iron (especially in menstruating women)
  • Magnesium (highly processed diets)
✅
Expert TipIf you suspect a deficiency, ask for a specific blood test rather than starting supplements blindly — over-supplementing certain nutrients can also cause problems.

A Grounded Approach

Most healthy adults get what they need from a varied diet. Supplements are useful for specific, identified gaps, best guided by an actual blood test rather than guesswork.

This article is for general educational purposes and is not a substitute for professional medical advice.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should everyone take a multivitamin?

Not necessarily — a varied diet covers most needs for most people; a multivitamin is more useful for specific, identified gaps or restrictive diets.

Can you take too much of a vitamin?

Yes — fat-soluble vitamins like A, D, E, and K can build up to harmful levels if significantly over-supplemented, unlike most water-soluble vitamins.

How do I know if I have a deficiency?

A simple blood test ordered by your doctor is the only reliable way to confirm a deficiency rather than guessing from symptoms alone.
